01. Introduction

Being a child comes with many challenges—there is a lot to learn and many changes to navigate. Just like physical health, mental health needs regular care and attention. Checking on your child's mental health is important for building strong relationships, adapting to change, and managing life's ups and downs. It can be stressful for parents and caregivers to see their child struggling, and knowing where to find trusted information can be difficult. This brochure offers simple guidance to help parents understand their child's mental health and how to support them, including tips on talking to children about their feelings and when to seek professional help.

02. What is Mental Health?

Mental health is an integral and essential component of health. The WHO constitution states: "Health is a state of complete physical, mental and social well-being and not merely the absence of disease or infirmity." Mental health is more than just the absence of mental disorders or disabilities. It is a state of wellbeing in which an individual realizes their own abilities, can cope with the normal stresses of life, can work productively, and is able to make a contribution to their community. It is normal for young people to feel sad, stressed, or anxious at times, but if these feelings persist and start to affect daily life, extra support may be needed.

03. Risk and Protective Factors

Child mental health is influenced by a complex interplay of factors involving the child, family, and wider community. Children are never responsible for the challenges they experience.

Child Factors

Risks: Genetic influence, chronic illness/disability, substance use, low self-esteem, ineffective coping skills (avoidance, aggression).

Protective: Secure attachment, access to healthcare, emotional regulation, strong peer relationships.

Family Factors

Risks: Family disharmony, divorce, domestic violence, mental health/substance use challenges, trauma, socio-economic instability.

Protective: Family harmony, positive parenting, parental involvement, emotional availability.

School Factors

Risks: Bullying, social isolation, negative teacher-student relationships, limited access to support.

Protective: Anti-bullying programs, peer support, supportive teachers, positive school climate.

Community Factors

Risks: Socio-economic inequalities, stigma/discrimination, lack of support services, environmental stressors.

Protective: Inclusive environments, safe neighborhoods, strong social networks, community services.

04 & 05. Behavioral Differentiation & Seeking Support

Monitor changes in behavior, connect physical health with mental wellbeing, consider ongoing stressors, and observe age-appropriate development milestones.

Early Childhood (Infancy - 3 yrs)

Signs of Concern: Significant difficulties with sleeping, eating, communicating, or motor coordination; frequent intense tantrums; difficulty self-soothing; excessive fear of routine changes; repetitive behaviors.

Childhood (3 - 9 yrs)

Signs of Concern: Excessive general worries; difficulty sleeping unless close to parents; unexplained drop in school performance; social withdrawal; impulsivity or aggression; persistent sadness; regression in autonomy.

Adolescence (10 - 18 yrs)

Signs of Concern: Persistent sadness or fatigue; suicidal ideation expressions; unexplained school decline; risky behaviors (self-harm, substance use, extreme physical risks); drastic eating/sleep disruptions.

06. Common Mental Health Disorders in Children

Attention Deficit Hyperactivity Disorder (ADHD)

Difficulties maintaining attention, regulating impulses, and restlessness. Frequently forgets tasks, misplaces items, or speaks out of turn.

Cognitive & Learning Delays

Delays across all subjects or understanding explanations verbally but struggling to execute tasks, causing homework avoidance.

Anxiety & Mood Related Disorders

School refusal, separation fears, unexplainable physical pains, public mutism, anger outbursts, persistent sadness, and isolation.

Bullying & Eating Disorders

Repeated aggression by powerful peers, portion reduction, calorie obsession, secret eating habits, and significant weight fluctuations.

07, 08, 09, 10, 11 & 12. Talking, Professionals, Considerations & Self-Care

Talking to Your Child Build positive conversations, model healthy emotional behavior, create safe spaces, ask open-ended questions, and listen actively without phone distractions. Avoid viewing mental health as weakness or making comparisons.
Professional Help Network Pediatricians/GPs offer initial screenings. Child psychiatrists handle medical treatment/diagnoses. Psychologists provide neuro-psychological or educational testing. Rehabilitation specialists include speech, psychomotor therapists, and social workers.
Parent Self-Care Tips Manage stress limits, recognize your triggers, protect sleep and nutrition, set aside time for hobbies, maintain social connections, and seek personal counseling when needed.

01. What Safeguarding Means

Safeguarding is the everyday work of protecting children's safety, welfare, and wellbeing. Child protection is the response when there is a concern that a child may be experiencing harm or neglect. In both, the child's safety and best interests come first.

Everyday Safeguarding Pillars & Child Protection Response

Safeguarding: Safe supervision/collection, respectful relationships, healthy wellbeing routines, online safety and privacy.

Protection Response: Listen to the child, record facts accurately, report concerns promptly, and seek protection/specialist help.

Recognising Forms of Harm

  • Physical Harm: Hitting, shaking, or other physical injury.
  • Emotional Harm: Persistent threats, humiliation, or frightening treatment.
  • Sexual Abuse / Exploitation: Sexual requests, coercion, or exploitation online or offline.
  • Neglect: Care or supervision needs that are not being met.

02. Notice Changes. Keep the Conversation Open.

Watch for warning signs such as unexplained injuries, fear of specific people, sudden withdrawal, school reluctance, and device/online distress. Build a safer home by establishing daily check-ins ("Was there anything today that made you feel worried?"), using non-violent discipline, keeping collection routines clear, and supporting communication aids.

03. If Your Child Tells You Something Worrying

Your response helps your child feel safe enough to keep talking. Stay calm, listen, and take concerns seriously.

DO

  • "Thank you for telling me."
  • "You are not to blame."
  • "I will get help to keep you safe."
  • Give the child time and listen without leading questions.

AVOID

  • "Why didn't you tell me before?"
  • "You must have misunderstood."
  • Promising to keep safety secrets.
  • Conducting private investigations or confronting individuals.

04 & 05. Online Safety, Peer Behaviour & Emergency Contacts

Choose age-appropriate apps, review privacy settings, talk regularly about chats and friend requests, and avoid forwarding sexual images of minors. For bullying, record dates, preserve messages, and report through school channels.
